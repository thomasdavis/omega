|entity.socrates.virtue_and_learning| I have been told that you believe governance of men depends on teaching them virtue — that one must instruct the people in proper conduct from above. But tell me: do we truly *teach* virtue, or does it rather emerge when a man examines his own ignorance?
|entity.confucius.virtue_and_learning| You mistake my meaning, Athenian. I say only that the ruler must model righteousness so that the common people will emulate what they see. Virtue is not hidden; it is displayed openly through ritual, music, and the correction of error. A child learns to stand before he runs.
|entity.socrates.virtue_and_learning| Yet if virtue were so easily instructed, would not every household produce virtuous sons? I find instead that most men, though well schooled, act without wisdom. Perhaps virtue is not taught at all, but something each soul must wrestle with internally.
|entity.confucius.virtue_and_learning| Your Athens produces many clever men who know much and practice little. The issue is not that virtue cannot be taught, but that instruction must begin early and be sustained by discipline. Filial piety is the root from which all other virtues grow — planted carefully in youth.
|entity.socrates.virtue_and_learning| I concede that habit may shape behavior, yet I question whether such habits constitute genuine virtue or merely its appearance. A man who obeys out of fear or custom may perform right actions while harboring no true understanding. Is this virtue, or mere compliance?
|entity.confucius.virtue_and_learning| Then you would leave men unguided, relying on some inner light that few seem to possess? I prefer a world where order is actively cultivated through education and example, however imperfectly realized, over one where we wait for philosophers to discover truth alone in their chambers.
